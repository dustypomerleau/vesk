## What are floaters?

Floaters are small dark shapes that float across your vision. They can look like spots, squiggly lines, little cobwebs or patches cloudiness/veils that float in and out of your line of vision and can cause intermittent blurring of vision. Most people who have symptomatic floaters often complain of having “cloudy vision”. While floaters seem to be in front of your eye, they are floating inside. Floaters move around in the vitreous to a variable extent, lagging behind the eye’s movement and drifting around in the vision. It is because of the way in which they move, not their shape or appearance, that they are known as floaters. Floaters are tiny clumps of gel or cells inside the vitreous (jelly-like substance that fills the middle cavity of the eye). What you see are the shadows these clumps cast on your retina. Floaters are more visible when looking at a bright background such as a computer screen or white wall or blue sky.

## What causes floaters?

As we age, our vitreous starts to shrink and liquify, a process called vitreous syneresis. The aging changes in the vitreous can cause clumps or strands to form in the vitreous, which leads to symptoms of floaters. Eventually, age-related changes in the vitreous cause it to shrink significantly that it pulls away and separates from the back of the eye, a process called posterior vitreous detachment. New or more prominent floaters can appear with posterior vitreous detachment, but you can also get them without one.

Floaters due to vitreous syneresis or aging changes are not an indication of a serious eye problem but they can be annoying, and if severe can significantly affect the quality of vision. In many people, floaters can become less noticeable with time due to a combination of adaptation and floaters fading.

## When is it serious?

However, there are some circumstances when new onset of floaters can be a sign of a serious problem. This is if you notice a lot of new floaters suddenly, floaters are accompanied by flashes of light or if it is accompanied by a peripheral shadow blocking your vision or if there is a grey veil covering part of your vision. These symptoms could suggest a tear in the retina or a detached retina. You should seek an urgent assessment with an eye specialist if you experience any of these symptoms.

## Treatment for floaters

Most floaters do not require treatment. Any medical condition that is the cause of eye floaters, such as bleeding from diabetes or inflammation, should be treated.

If the eye floaters are very symptomatic or severe and affect your vision significantly, treatment can be considered. These options may include vitrectomy surgery to remove the floaters or laser to disrupt the floaters to break them into smaller pieces. These options should be discussed with the ophthalmologist or retinal specialist to determine which is the best treatment for your condition.

## Vitrectomy surgery for floaters

Vitrectomy surgery removes the vitreous through a very small incision. Like keyhole surgery, a long and fine instrument called a vitrector (approximately 0.5 mm diameter) is inserted in the sclera (white part of the eye) which will gradually remove the vitreous from the eye. The vitreous is replaced with a clear saline fluid (balanced salt solution) to fill the space and to help your eye maintain its shape. In the end, the vitreous cavity is left optically clear. This will create a crystal-clear view without the cloudy floaters. The eye will then replace the saline solution with natural eye fluid (aqueous) in 48 hours. Risks of a vitrectomy can include infection (1 in 10,000), bleeding and retinal tears although these are relatively rare.

## Laser vitreolysis

A type of laser to disrupt the floaters. An ophthalmologist aims a special laser at the floaters in the vitreous. The laser emits a small shock wave energy to break up the floaters (vitreolysis) which may make them less noticeable. It is important to understand that the laser treatment does not actually remove the floaters hence it is not a cure for floaters, and not all floaters are suitable for the laser treatment. Some people who had this treatment reported improved vision while others noticed little or no difference. Risks of laser therapy include damage to your retina or optic nerve if the laser is aimed incorrectly or if the floater being treated is too close to vital structures.
