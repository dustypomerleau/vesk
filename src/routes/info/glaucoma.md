Glaucoma is a disease of the optic nerve, often associated with raised intraocular pressures. However, those with intraocular pressures in the normal range can still develop glaucomatous optic nerve damage. This damage results in tunnel vision and in at the very end stage can lead to complete blindness. The damage occurs slowly and typically takes many years to cause blindness.

The higher the intraocular pressure, the more likely the patient is to develop glaucoma. Patients with intraocular pressures of 35 or more all develop glaucoma. Of those whose pressures are consistently around 30, approximately 80% develop glaucoma, while patients with pressures around 25% have a 25% risk of glaucoma, and pressures of 23 have a 10% risk.

The risk of glaucoma may be increased with family history of glaucoma, age over 50, previous eye injury, history of diabetes, cortisone use and high blood pressure.

## Diagnostic investigations

Diagnosing glaucoma can be difficult and often the diagnosis can only be confirmed after several years of monitoring. Regular tests include intraocular pressure measurement, examination of the drainage angle, optic nerve examination, visual field testing, OCT of the optic nerve, and occasionally MRI or CT scan of the brain and orbits.

## Types of glaucoma

1. Primary open angle glaucoma.
   This is the most common form, causing over 90% of glaucoma in the western countries. This causes slow, painless loss of peripheral vision. A family history of glaucoma can indicate increased risk or severity.
1. Acute angle closure glaucoma.
   This form occurs more commonly in the Asian population. It is due to the anatomy of the eye resulting in narrow drainage angles, predisposing to a risk of acute spikes of eye pressure. When acute risk of eye pressure occurs, this is painful and can cause acute vision loss. This requires immediate medical intervention to save the sight.
1. Secondary open angle or angle closure glaucoma.
   This occurs with another condition is contributing to the condition, such as diabetes, retinal vein occlusion, pseudoexfoliation or trauma.
1. Normal tension glaucoma.
   This type of glaucoma shows typical progressive damage of the optic nerve with loss of peripheral vision, but the intraocular pressure remains within normal limits, often under 21.

## Treatment

Treatment is best commenced early, when there is minimal damage that is only detectable by examination techniques and not noticed by the patient. Treatment prevents worsening of the damage to the optic nerve and vision, but does not reverse any damage that has already occurred.

If there is mild damage, the treatment is typically successful at preventing further damage and preserving vision. If treatment is commenced late when there is moderate or severe damage , the treatment is less successful.

Glaucoma is treated by lowering intraocular pressure. The first line of treatment is usually with pressure-lowering eye drops. Laser and surgery to lower the eye pressure is considered if there is suboptimal effect or inadequate eye pressure control with eye drops. Over 95% of patients require only eye drops, particularly if treatment is started early.

## Glaucoma suspect

A “ glaucoma suspect” is someone who has some features of glaucoma, but not sufficient to warrant treatment. Glaucoma suspects include patients who have the following:

- Raised intraocular pressure (range 20-30), but other tests and examination are normal
- Abnormal looking optic nerve, but other findings are normal.
- Slightly abnormal visual field testing, but other findings are normal.
- Positive family history together with one of the above features.

Glaucoma suspects require regular checkups with visual field testing and optic nerve assessment for several years, usually on an annual basis. This is to idenfity any signs of progression or worsening that would then confirm the diagnosis of glaucoma.
